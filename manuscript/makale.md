## Highlights
- The P1 to P4 gap is a population effect: unseen creators score 0.898 even in P1.
- Creator grouping alone does not inflate scores when reputation is causal.
- Published creator reputation tracks a causal rebuild, not a look-ahead one.
- On unseen creators, LightGBM beats the best one-feature rule by 0.107 PR-AUC.
- Half of the rugs come within 26 s; for rugs a minute away PR-AUC is 0.746.

## Abstract
pump.fun on Solana creates tens of thousands of tokens daily, most of which collapse within minutes. Detectors that flag likely rug pulls within seconds are increasingly proposed, but their evaluations rarely account for serial creators, track-record features that encode future outcomes, and rule-based labels. We audit early rug-pull prediction on a public CC BY 4.0 table of pump.fun launches (924,742 launches by 254,537 creators over 26 days). We define a protocol ladder from random-mint splits (P1) to time-forward splits scored only on creators unseen in training (P4). An embargoed causal creator reputation audits the published reputation fields against a look-ahead reconstruction, and four learners are compared with one-feature rules under creator-clustered bootstrap tests. LightGBM PR-AUC falls from 0.957 (95% CI 0.953–0.962) under P1 to 0.923 (0.909–0.934) under P4, whereas creator grouping alone changes nothing. The gap reflects who is tested: inside the P1 test set, launches by creators absent from training score 0.898. The published reputation agrees more often with the causal reconstruction (57.0% of launches) than with the look-ahead one (42.4%) and gives no performance gain. Under P4, the learner beats the best one-feature rule by 0.107 PR-AUC (0.097–0.117). Rankings hold across six label definitions, calibration is good (ECE 0.006) and a frozen model shows no 12-day trend. Half of the rugs occur within 26 s of the snapshot, and for rugs at least 60 s away PR-AUC is 0.746 at prevalence 0.376. Classic-trained models rank mayhem-mode launches poorly (ROC-AUC 0.735); pooled training repairs this. Code reproduces every number.

**Keywords:** rug pull; memecoin; Solana; data leakage; temporal validation; fraud detection; calibration

## 1. Introduction
Launchpads have changed how cryptocurrency tokens are issued. On pump.fun, anyone can create a Solana token in a single transaction. The token trades against a bonding curve until roughly 85 SOL of real reserves have been raised, and it then migrates to a decentralised exchange pool. Creation is cheap, so the platform produces a very large stream of short-lived tokens. Many of them follow a familiar pattern (Li et al., 2026; Chen et al., 2026b): the creator or a group of coordinated insiders sells into early buyers and the price collapses. The security literature calls such exits rug pulls (Mazorra et al., 2022; Sun et al., 2025). Unlike the backdoored Ethereum contracts studied earlier (Xia et al., 2021; Zhou et al., 2024; Yu and Lee, 2025; Cai et al., 2026), launchpad tokens share audited template code. Detection must therefore rely on market behaviour within the first seconds to minutes after launch (Li et al., 2025; Kalacheva et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026).

Several recent studies report that gradient-boosted trees or graph neural networks can separate rug pulls from other launches with high accuracy (Wu et al., 2025; Srifa et al., 2025; Kalacheva et al., 2026; Li et al., 2026; Hu et al., 2026). The headline numbers come from evaluation designs that are standard in tabular machine learning, but three properties of the launchpad setting make those designs fragile. First, launches are not independent. A creator wallet can launch many tokens at negligible cost, so a random split places the same creator on both sides and a model can learn who launched a token rather than how it trades. This is a form of group leakage (Roberts et al., 2017; Kapoor and Narayanan, 2023). Second, creator track-record features such as "share of the creator's earlier launches that were rugged" are only causal if each earlier outcome was already resolved at the moment of the new launch. A track record computed from the final dataset can quietly import the future. This matches the data-snooping and label-leakage pitfalls documented for machine learning in computer security (Arp et al., 2024). Third, rug labels are observation rules (for example, a drawdown below a share of the running peak, or a creator dump) rather than adjudicated fraud. The data-collection pipeline also changes over time through decoder fixes, capture outages and new platform modes. Performance can then reflect the rule or the pipeline rather than fraud (Geirhos et al., 2020; Gama et al., 2014).

This paper asks how much of the reported ability to predict pump.fun rug pulls within seconds survives an evaluation that controls these three channels. We make four contributions.

- **A protocol ladder** (P1–P4) that moves from random-mint splits to creator-grouped splits, forward-chaining time splits with a purge gap, and time-forward splits evaluated only on creators never seen in training. The gap between rungs quantifies creator and temporal leakage.
- **A causal, embargoed creator reputation** computed by an O(n log n) event sweep, together with an audit that tests whether published reputation fields agree more with a causal reconstruction or with a look-ahead (oracle) reconstruction.
- **A baseline-first comparison** of four learners against a prevalence predictor and one-feature rules (creator reputation, developer share, insider share, mayhem flag). It uses creator-clustered bootstrap intervals, DeLong and McNemar tests, Benjamini–Hochberg control, Friedman rank tests across folds, calibration analysis and daily drift.
- **Stress tests and open code**: label horizon and branch, mayhem versus classic launches, decoder and multi-hop seams, capture silence, transfer from sampled legacy features, first-time versus repeat creators and seeds. All are computed from a public CC BY 4.0 dataset with documented known issues.

We pre-specified four hypotheses. H1: PR-AUC under P1 exceeds PR-AUC under P4 (protocol inflation). H2: the published reputation fields are closer to the oracle than to the causal reconstruction (look-ahead). H3: under P4, the reference learner (LightGBM, fixed in advance) beats the best one-feature rule by a margin whose creator-clustered 95% interval excludes zero. H4: the ranking of methods is stable across label definitions. Section 5 reports each hypothesis together with its decision rule. Negative outcomes are reported as such. In brief, H1, H3 and H4 are supported and H2 is not. H1 holds by its decision rule, but the gap reflects which creators are tested: launches by new creators score no higher under P1 than under P4. Unlike Li et al. (2026), who test on rolling time windows without holding out creators, and Heydaridoost et al. (2026), who split chronologically with a label embargo but use no track-record features, we test on creators unseen in training, audit published track records for look-ahead, and compare learners with one-feature rules under creator-clustered tests.

## 2. Related work
**Rug-pull detection on account-based chains.** Early work characterised scam tokens on Uniswap (Xia et al., 2021) and machine-learning detection of rug pulls from liquidity and holder features (Mazorra et al., 2022). Contract-level analyses expose backdoors and risky permissions (Zhou et al., 2024; Yu and Lee, 2025; Cai et al., 2026). A systematisation of knowledge reviews rug-pull types, datasets and tools (Sun et al., 2025). Temporal graph models (Wu et al., 2025), transaction-based classifiers on decentralised exchanges (Srifa et al., 2025) and multi-agent LLM pipelines (Chen et al., 2026a) extend this line, and interpretable feature-importance studies examine on-chain indicators (Hardiansyah et al., 2026).

**Solana and launchpad memecoins.** SolRPDS provides a dataset of rug pulls in Solana decentralised finance (Alhaidari et al., 2025). Li et al. (2025) study trust dynamics and bot-driven responses to rug pulls in Solana memecoin markets. Cordoba Otalora (2025) proposes a pump.fun metric for subculture-driven speculation. Kalacheva et al. (2026) analyse rug pulls on decentralised exchanges in the memecoin era. Li et al. (2026) assemble 6.4 million Solana tokens over seven months and report that gradient boosting detects likely rug pulls from the first five minutes of trading, with cross-platform shift between pump.fun and Raydium. MELT (Hu et al., 2026) offers 41k+ launch traces with bundle links, 122 behavioural features and risk-level annotations. TM-RugPull (Shoaei et al., 2026a) curates 1,000 manually labelled projects with a strictly time-bound design. Chen et al. (2026b) label 100,063 Solana DEX tokens with three behavioural rules and a manual audit, and trace 78 large scam groups, most of them multi-party syndicates. Kamat (2026a) studies sniper cohorts and filter rejections across two windows; a companion study finds that two observation pipelines on the same launchpad see almost disjoint token sets (Kamat, 2026b). On pump.fun itself, Marino et al. (2026) estimate graduation probabilities causally along the bonding curve, and Gafarov (2026) finds that collapses cluster in creator-linked teams, mostly sold by a single creator wallet. Heydaridoost et al. (2026) split 2,700 SolRPDS tokens chronologically with a label embargo and find that graph neural networks do not beat XGBoost at cutoffs of 12.5 to 93 minutes.

**Evaluation pitfalls.** Arp et al. (2024) list sampling bias, label inaccuracy, data snooping, spurious correlations and inappropriate baselines as recurrent pitfalls in security ML. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023) show how leakage drives a reproducibility crisis across ML-based science. Grouped and blocked cross-validation for dependent data (Roberts et al., 2017), forward evaluation for temporal data (Bergmeir and Benítez, 2012; Cerqueira et al., 2020), concept drift (Gama et al., 2014), shortcut learning (Geirhos et al., 2020), and metric choice under imbalance (Saito and Rehmsmeier, 2015; Chicco and Jurman, 2020) and calibration (Niculescu-Mizil and Caruana, 2005) inform our design. On Ethereum and BSC, the LROO detector extracts every feature before the liquidity withdrawal and tests on a time-segmented hold-out (Shoaei et al., 2026b); it does not separate deployers between training and test. Our previous work applied a leakage audit to Turkish phishing e-mail corpora (Çetlenbik and Süzen, 2026). The present paper carries that perspective to on-chain fraud.

**Gap.** Time-ordered evaluation is no longer rare: Li et al. (2026) use rolling forward windows, MELT splits launches 7:3 by creation time, TM-RugPull is time-bound by design, and Heydaridoost et al. (2026) add a label embargo. To our reading, however, none of the Solana memecoin studies evaluates on creators unseen in training, audits creator track-record features for look-ahead, compares learners against one-feature rules with clustered significance tests, or reports calibration and day-by-day drift. Table 1 summarises the positioning. The comparison is restricted to what each paper reports and does not judge the quality of their datasets, which our study builds on.

## 3. Data
### 3.1 Source, licence and provenance
We use the public dataset *pump.fun launches with outcomes* (loopholetape, 2026), released on Hugging Face under CC BY 4.0. No account, key or payment is needed to download the parquet files. The vendor also runs a paid live API, which we do not use. According to its card, the dataset is assembled by an automated agent from Solana transaction data. It contains one row per pump.fun launch between 2026-08-31 and 2026-10-22 (2,099,927 launches) in two subsets: `launches` (1,240,719 rows from 2026-09-27, after a decoder fix) and `launches_legacy` (earlier rows with an incomplete decoder). We pin the release by commit hash in the code (`config.HF_REVISION` = 022649eb0504005be0ae34833d224341777355be, last modified 29 October 2026) and verify every downloaded day file against the SHA-256 listed in the card; all 53 day files of the two subsets used here matched. The 2,099,927 launches split into 1,240,719 `launches` rows (26 day files, 2026-09-27 to 2026-10-22) and 859,208 `launches_legacy` rows (27 day files, 2026-08-31 to 2026-09-26), which matches the card. Other descriptive statistics quoted in this subsection come from the dataset card and are not our results.

As a light independent check of provenance we queried the public Solana mainnet RPC endpoint (read-only, no key) for eight launches drawn at random with the study seed (`code/crosscheck_rpc.py`, results/crosscheck_rpc.csv). For all eight, the fee payer of the mint's oldest transaction equals the dataset's creator wallet, and that transaction's block time lies 0.36–1.43 s before the dataset's launch timestamp, consistent with block times being whole seconds and the dataset recording capture receipt. All eight mints are Token-2022 accounts with six decimals and no mint authority. This check covers identity and timing only; we did not re-derive rug labels from the chain.

### 3.2 Labels are observation rules
The labels are not verified fraud. The card defines graduation as completion of the bonding curve (≈85 SOL) or an observed migration after 80 SOL. Against the chain's own completion flag, it reports a recall of 98.67% and a precision of 99.85% for this rule. A launch is labelled *rugged* if either of two rules fires. *DRAIN*: after at least 5 SOL of real reserves, the curve falls to 25% or less of its running peak before migration. *DEV DUMP*: the creator sells at least half of its peak holding within 300 s and the curve is below half its peak at that moment. Our primary target, *rug within 300 s*, is rugged = 1 and a rug time of at most 300 s after launch. We additionally require the rug to occur strictly after the decision-time snapshot, so that a model is never asked to predict an event it has already observed. Because the labels are rules over the same reserve trajectory that early features summarise, we treat label definition as a robustness dimension (Section 4.6). We also report an alternative-rule variant, *collapse90*. It is not independent of the reserve path, but it does not use the creator-dump branch or the 25% threshold: terminal reserves at 10% or less of the peak, clipped at 85.1 SOL, for launches whose peak reached at least 1 SOL. The DRAIN/DEV DUMP branch used in robustness runs is the dataset's inferred branch field (`rug_branch_inferred`). Its second level is named `dev_dump_or_recovered`, which suggests it pools creator dumps with paths classified as recovered. We therefore interpret the DEV DUMP-only run as an upper bound of that branch, not a clean creator-dump label.

### 3.3 Decision-time features
Predictions are issued at the first snapshot taken less than 10 s after launch (`f10_*`). The features are the creator's share of supply after its first buy, the insider share, the number of bundle wallets, real SOL reserves, the number of buys, the snapshot age, the size of the developer's initial buy, and the mayhem-mode flag. The dataset also publishes three creator track-record fields at that snapshot (`f10_rep_n`, `f10_rep_rug`, `f10_rep_grad`). Section 4.2 audits them. We never use the free-text fields `name`, `symbol` and `uri`. They are attacker-controlled, the card warns that they may contain injected instructions (KI-14), and they could leak identity. We report no wallet addresses and publish no per-wallet risk scores; creator wallets are pseudonymous and only public blockchain-derived data aggregated by a third party are used.

### 3.4 Cleaning according to known issues
The card documents 18 known issues. We act on the following ones.

- We keep SOL-quoted launches only (KI-03).
- We split on the decoder seam (KI-02): the main analysis uses `launches` with `decoder_complete = 1`, and the legacy subset is used only for transfer and selection-bias analyses.
- Unresolved outcomes are excluded, never set to zero (KI-08).
- Launches without an `f10` snapshot are excluded, since features exist for a biased subset in the legacy period (KI-01). Launches whose creation was observed more than 1 s late are also excluded.
- Peaks are clipped at 85.1 SOL (KI-12).
- Mayhem-mode launches are analysed both pooled (with a flag) and separately (KI-04). Mayhem mode is a launch mode offered by the platform during the study window and flagged in the dataset. We use only the flag and make no assumption about its internal mechanics.
- Launches inside the multi-hop seam window (KI-18) are flagged and excluded in a robustness run.

Table 2 reports the exclusion flow (results/flow.csv). The largest losses come from non-SOL quotes (224,672 launches) and missing `f10` snapshots (53,682). Removing launches that had already rugged (15,773) or migrated (10,520) before the snapshot keeps the task predictive rather than descriptive.

## 4. Methods
![Figure 1. Study design. (a) Decision time, label window and causal creator history for one launch. (b) The four evaluation protocols. Conceptual schematic; contains no data.](figures/fig1_design_schematic.png)

### 4.1 Evaluation protocols
Each row is one launch, so a random split by row is a random split by mint. **P1 (random mint)** holds out 20% of launches at random. **P2 (creator-grouped)** holds out 20% of creators, with all of their launches. **P3 (time-forward)** forms forward-chaining folds over UTC days: 14 training days, a 1-day purge gap and 2 test days, advancing by 2 days. With 26 complete-decoder days this gives at most five folds (the exact folds are written to results/folds.csv). The last fold is the *hold-out* used in E2–E5. Its last two training days serve as validation days for recalibration only. The purge exceeds the 1 h path-closure time after which outcomes settle. **P4 (time-forward, unseen creators)** uses the P3 folds but scores only test launches whose creator does not appear in the training window. P4 is the deployment-relevant setting for a protection tool that must judge launches by new wallets. Rule thresholds, imputers and scalers are fitted on training data only. The operating point for threshold metrics (MCC, McNemar) flags the same share of test launches as the training prevalence. It uses the test score distribution but no test labels, so it is equivalent to a fixed alert budget. Budget-based precision at 1%, 5% and 10% is reported alongside it. Section 5.9 repeats the operating points with thresholds fixed on the validation days.

### 4.2 Causal creator reputation and look-ahead audit
For launch *i* by creator *c* at time *t₀ᵢ*, an earlier launch *j* by *c* counts as resolved only if its resolution time is strictly before *t₀ᵢ*. Launch *j* resolves at *t₀ⱼ* + min(*rug_dtⱼ*, *E*), where the embargo *E* = 3,600 s is a conservative stand-in for the dataset's path closure. It is credited as rugged if it rugged within *E*, and as graduated if it migrated by its resolution time. A launch that rugs only after the embargo therefore counts as not rugged from *t₀ⱼ* + *E* on, exactly like a launch that never rugs, so whether *j* enters the history never depends on its future. Unresolved launches never count. The causal reputation is the count of earlier launches, the count of resolved earlier launches, and the rug and graduation shares among the resolved ones (missing when none). We compute it over all SOL-quoted launches of both subsets (so that legacy launches count as history) with a single vectorised event sweep: resolution events and launch queries are sorted by creator and time, with queries placed first on ties, and per-creator cumulative sums are read at the queries. The cost is O(n log n). An *oracle* reconstruction credits every earlier launch with the same labels at its own launch time. It is causal in ordering but not in knowledge, and it differs from the causal version only in when an outcome becomes known. The audit (E2) compares the published fields with both reconstructions through exact-agreement share, mean absolute difference and correlation. It then compares downstream performance with no reputation, causal, published and oracle reputation, each as the same five-column feature group. If the published fields match the oracle more closely than the causal version and inflate performance, they leak future information. Our unit tests check the sweep against a brute-force O(n²) implementation, including launches that rug after the embargo. Because E is a modelling choice, E2b repeats the causal-reputation analysis with E = 600 s and E = 86,400 s. Both the reputation rule and the reference learner are refitted.

### 4.3 Models and baselines
The baselines are a prevalence predictor and four one-feature rules: causal creator rug share, creator share of supply, insider share, and the mayhem flag. Each rule is turned into a calibrated score by binning the feature into training quantiles, with missing values kept as their own bin, and returning the shrunken training rug rate of each bin. The learners are L2-regularised logistic regression (C = 1) on log-transformed, median-imputed and standardised features; a random forest (Breiman, 2001) with 300 trees and a minimum leaf size of 50; histogram gradient boosting; and LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017), a gradient-boosting implementation (Friedman, 2001; Chen and Guestrin, 2016), with 600 trees, 63 leaves, a minimum of 200 samples per leaf and row and column subsampling of 0.8. We deliberately restrict the learners to tabular models on the published decision-time snapshot. Sequence and graph models (Wu et al., 2025) need the full transaction stream, which is not part of the public table. The audit question is whether *any* learner beats simple rules under leakage controls, not which architecture is best. Hyperparameters were fixed before any test data were seen. LightGBM is designated in advance as the *reference learner* for all paired tests and robustness runs; it is not chosen by test performance. If LightGBM is not installed, the code falls back to histogram gradient boosting and records this. The global seed is 20261030.

### 4.4 Metrics
Prevalence differs strongly across label variants and regimes (it is reported per analysis), so we report PR-AUC (average precision) of the rug class together with its lift over prevalence, ROC-AUC, the Matthews correlation coefficient (Chicco and Jurman, 2020), the Brier score, the expected calibration error with ten equal-width bins, and precision at alert budgets of 1%, 5% and 10% of launches. Launches tied at the cut-off score enter the budget in expectation, so that a constant or binned score does not gain from row order. We prefer PR-based and calibration metrics to accuracy (Saito and Rehmsmeier, 2015).

### 4.5 Statistical inference
Launches by the same creator are dependent, so all intervals come from a creator-clustered percentile bootstrap with 2,000 replicates: creators are resampled with replacement. Paired differences between two scorers on the same test set use the same clustered bootstrap for PR-AUC, the DeLong test for correlated ROC-AUCs (DeLong et al., 1988), and an exact McNemar test on threshold decisions (Dietterich, 1998). Multiplicity is controlled with the Benjamini–Hochberg procedure (Benjamini and Hochberg, 1995) within pre-specified families. The primary family is the clustered-bootstrap PR-AUC test over all E3 pairwise contrasts and E4 ablations. DeLong and McNemar p-values form two separate secondary families. Robustness runs (E5) are descriptive and are not tested. Methods are compared across forward folds with the Friedman rank test (Friedman, 1937). With at most five folds, whose training windows overlap, the Friedman test has low power and is read descriptively. Time-forward folds also give a dispersion estimate that respects temporal order (Bergmeir and Benítez, 2012; Cerqueira et al., 2020). DeLong and McNemar assume independent launches and are reported as secondary to the clustered bootstrap. When no bootstrap replicate crosses zero we report p < 0.001, because 2,000 replicates cannot resolve smaller values.

### 4.6 Robustness and ablation
The ablations (E4) remove, one group at a time, creator reputation, holder structure (creator, insider and bundle shares), early order flow (reserves, buys, snapshot age, developer buy) and the mayhem flag. The robustness runs (E5) are the following.

- Label horizons of 60 s, 300 s and 3,600 s, any rug, and the DRAIN and DEV DUMP branches separately. The alternative-rule *collapse90* label also tests H4.
- Regime shift: classic→classic, classic→mayhem and mayhem→mayhem fits.
- Excluding launches with capture silence in the first 3 s.
- Excluding the multi-hop seam window from training.
- Separate evaluation for first-time and repeat creators.
- Five seeds.
- Training on the legacy subset, separately for replayed and sampled features (KI-01), and testing on the complete-decoder hold-out. In the pinned release every legacy launch with features has `features_basis = harness_sample`, so only the sampled variant could be run.

Finally (E6), permutation importance measured as a PR-AUC drop is compared between P1 and P4 with Kendall's τ, to test whether explanations change once leakage is removed (Lundberg et al., 2020; Rudin, 2019). Several features are correlated, for example the two developer-share fields and insider share with bundle wallets. Single-feature permutation then understates the importance of a correlated group, so the group-level ablations in E4 are the primary evidence on feature groups, and E6 is read as a rank comparison only.

## 5. Results
Code, per-run results and the manuscript source are available under the MIT licence at https://github.com/osmancancet/pumpfun-rugpull-audit. All numbers in this section come from the files named in brackets, produced by `code/run_all.sh` (pre-specified analyses) and `code/s04_supplementary.py` (two post hoc analyses added during internal review round 3, marked as such). Unless stated otherwise, the hold-out is the last forward fold: training days 2026-10-05 to 10-18, a one-day gap, and test days 10-20 and 10-21 (61,840 launches, rug prevalence 0.686).

### 5.1 Cohort
After cleaning, the analysis cohort contains 924,742 launches by 254,537 creator wallets (results/flow.csv, results/table1_descriptives.csv). Of these, 638,024 are classic launches by 247,665 creators and 286,718 are mayhem-mode launches by only 13,932 creators (7,060 wallets launched in both modes). Within 300 s of the snapshot, 57.4% of classic and 88.8% of mayhem launches meet the rug label; 1.29% of classic and 0.016% of mayhem launches graduate. First launches of a wallet are 36.1% of classic but only 2.7% of mayhem launches. Launching is extremely concentrated (results/creator_concentration.csv): the median creator made one launch, the most active wallet made 10,006, and the top 1% of creators account for 49.7% of all launches. This concentration is what makes creator-level leakage possible.

### 5.2 Protocol inflation (H1)
Decision rule: H1 is supported if the creator-clustered 95% interval of the reference learner's PR-AUC under P1 lies above that under P4.

With causal reputation, LightGBM reaches a PR-AUC of 0.957 [0.953, 0.962] under P1 and 0.923 [0.909, 0.934] on the P4 hold-out (Table 3, Figure 2; results/E1_protocol_CIs.csv). The P1 interval lies above the P4 interval of every one of the five forward folds (upper limits 0.926–0.941), so **H1 is supported by its decision rule**. With the published reputation fields the picture is the same (0.955 [0.951, 0.960] versus 0.918 [0.903, 0.931]).

Three qualifications matter for interpretation. First, the inflation is modest: about 0.035 PR-AUC and 0.02 ROC-AUC. For ROC-AUC, which does not depend on prevalence, the P1 interval [0.922, 0.934] and the P4 hold-out interval [0.898, 0.923] overlap slightly. Second, part of the PR-AUC drop is a composition effect. Launches by creators unseen in training rug less often (P4 prevalence 0.593 ± 0.020 over folds, versus 0.671 under P1), and PR-AUC falls with prevalence; the lift over prevalence actually rises from 1.43 to 1.56. Third, creator grouping alone causes no inflation: P2 gives 0.958 [0.945, 0.969], which is indistinguishable from P1, and P3 gives 0.953 ± 0.001. The drop appears only when the test set is restricted to unseen creators. With causal features, therefore, random splits mainly overstate performance on *new* creators rather than leaking identity through features.

A post hoc split of the test sets supports this reading (Table 6; results/R6_review_analyses.csv). Inside the P1 test set, the 42,091 launches whose creator does not appear in P1 training reach a PR-AUC of 0.898 [0.894, 0.902] at prevalence 0.577, while the 142,857 launches by seen creators reach 0.966. The same split of the P3 hold-out gives 0.967 for seen and 0.923 for unseen creators. Random-mint splits therefore do not score new creators better than time-forward splits do. They report a higher average because 77% of their test launches come from creators already in training. On classic launches only, the gap is 0.935 under P1 versus 0.909 under P4. Mayhem launches make up 31.1% of the P1 test set, 32.6% of the P3 hold-out and 23.5% of the P4 hold-out. We read H1 as a difference in test population, not as leakage of creator identity.

### 5.3 Reputation look-ahead audit (H2)
Decision rule: H2 is supported if the published `f10_rep_rug` agrees exactly with the oracle reconstruction for a larger share of launches than with the causal one, and if replacing causal reputation with published reputation raises hold-out PR-AUC by an amount whose clustered interval excludes zero.

**H2 is not supported on either criterion** (results/table5b_rep_agreement.md, results/table5_reputation.md, results/S1_reputation_diff.csv). Among the 647,746 launches where both the published and the causal rug share are defined, the published value equals the causal reconstruction for 57.0% (mean absolute error 0.021, Pearson r = 0.983). Among the 648,265 launches where the oracle share is defined, it equals the oracle for only 42.4% (MAE 0.039, r = 0.941). Replacing causal with published reputation *lowers* hold-out PR-AUC slightly, by 0.0017 [0.0006, 0.0027] (post hoc paired clustered bootstrap, round 3). The oracle reconstruction, which uses the same within-embargo outcomes but at their final state, before they are known at launch, raises PR-AUC by 0.0008 [0.0003, 0.0013] (p = 0.002). The look-ahead gain is detectable, but it is under 3% of what reputation contributes as a whole (removing it costs 0.027 PR-AUC; Section 5.6), and the published fields do not capture it.

The overall agreement shares include launches on which the two reconstructions coincide. On the 180,838 launches (27.9%) where the causal and oracle rug shares differ, the published value equals the causal one for 55.1% and the oracle one for 2.6%, and it is closer to the causal value for 68.9% (post hoc; results/R6_review_analyses.csv). The published field is therefore not a look-ahead quantity. It is not identical to our causal reconstruction either. Part of the remaining disagreement reflects a different rule for when an outcome counts, which this audit cannot pin down.

Neither reconstruction reproduces the published fields exactly, and this is itself a reproducibility finding. The published launch count `f10_rep_n` matches the oracle count of earlier launches more often (60.5%) than the causal count of *resolved* earlier launches (46.1%), with large mean absolute errors in both cases (79 and 83 launches). The most plausible reading is a definitional difference: the published count appears to include every earlier launch, resolved or not, and the provider's history may start before the first day of the public files. We cannot verify this from the public data and state it as a hypothesis.

Embargo sensitivity (E2b) separates the learner from the rule. LightGBM is almost unaffected by the embargo (PR-AUC 0.954 at E = 600 s, 0.953 at 3,600 s, 0.952 at 86,400 s). The one-feature reputation rule degrades as outcomes are withheld for longer: 0.909, 0.899 and 0.871, with ROC-AUC falling from 0.852 to 0.806.

### 5.4 Learners versus rules (H3)
Decision rule: H3 is supported if the reference learner beats every one-feature rule in PR-AUC with a BH-adjusted p-value below 0.05 and a clustered interval excluding zero.

On the hold-out, LightGBM reaches a PR-AUC of 0.953 (lift 1.39), ROC-AUC 0.919, MCC 0.660 and ECE 0.006 (Table 4; results/table3_models.md, results/table4_tests.md). The best rule is causal reputation, with a PR-AUC of 0.899, and LightGBM beats it by 0.054 [0.045, 0.063] (BH q < 0.001). Every other rule is further behind, and all DeLong and McNemar contrasts with rules have q < 0.001. Because H3 concerns new creators, we repeated the paired tests on the P4 hold-out (30,030 launches by unseen creators, prevalence 0.588) in round 3 (post hoc; results/S2_P4_holdout_tests.csv). There, the best rule is insider share (0.815), and LightGBM (0.923) beats it by 0.107 [0.097, 0.117]. The reputation rule falls to 0.769 (Δ = 0.154 [0.129, 0.182]), and all BH q < 0.001. **H3 is supported** under both P3 and P4. Across the five P4 folds, LightGBM ranks first in every fold (Friedman χ² = 40, df = 8, p < 0.001; mean ranks: histogram boosting 2, random forest 3, logistic regression 4, insider rule 5, creator-share rule 6, reputation rule 7, mayhem rule 8, prevalence 9; results/E3_friedman_P4.csv).

Two post hoc checks bound this margin (Table 6). On classic launches only, LightGBM beats insider share on the P4 hold-out by 0.082 [0.073, 0.092]. A logistic scorecard on causal reputation, its missing-history flag, creator share and insider share reaches 0.904 on the hold-out and 0.854 on P4. LightGBM beats it by 0.049 [0.043, 0.055] and 0.068 [0.060, 0.078] (both p < 0.001). The gap over a transparent multi-feature score is smaller than over the best single rule, but it does not close.

The learners themselves differ little. The gaps to random forest (0.002 [0.001, 0.003], q < 0.001) and histogram boosting (0.001 [0.000, 0.001], q = 0.046) are statistically detectable on 61,840 launches but have no practical meaning. Logistic regression is 0.022 [0.019, 0.026] behind. At an alert budget of the top 1% of launches, every learner is at least 98% precise, and so is the reputation rule (0.978).

### 5.5 Calibration and drift
The tree learners are already well calibrated (results/E3_calibration.csv, Figure 3). When recalibration is fitted on the two validation days only, LightGBM's ECE moves from 0.0104 (raw) to 0.0078 (isotonic) and 0.0076 (Platt), and its Brier score barely changes (0.0957 to 0.0958). Isotonic recalibration slightly lowers PR-AUC (0.951 to 0.950) because it creates ties. Only logistic regression gains from recalibration (ECE 0.024 to 0.014 with isotonic). These calibration models are trained without the validation days, so their PR-AUC differs slightly from Table 4.

A LightGBM model frozen after the first training window (2026-09-27 to 10-10) and scored on each later day from 10-11 to 10-22 (29,663–36,455 launches per day) shows no monotone decay (results/E3_daily_drift_frozen_model.csv, Figure 4). Daily PR-AUC stays between 0.943 and 0.958, ROC-AUC between 0.906 and 0.929, and ECE between 0.008 and 0.016, while daily prevalence moves between 0.652 and 0.725, with no monotone trend. The multi-hop seam (8–9 October) lies inside the frozen training window, so none of the scored days is a seam day. A least-squares line through the 12 daily PR-AUC values has a slope of −0.00012 per day [−0.00099, 0.00075]. For launches by creators absent from the frozen training window the slope is +0.0017 [−0.0002, 0.0035] (post hoc, t intervals). Neither interval excludes zero.

### 5.6 Ablation
Removing creator reputation costs 0.027 [0.022, 0.033] PR-AUC (q < 0.001) and lowers ROC-AUC from 0.919 to 0.881 (results/table4_tests.md, results/all_runs.csv). It is the only feature group whose removal matters in practice. Removing holder structure (0.001 [0.001, 0.002]), early order flow (0.001 [0.000, 0.002], q = 0.008) or the mayhem flag (0.000 [−0.000, 0.001], q = 0.555) changes PR-AUC by at most 0.0013. The groups are partly redundant: developer buy size, developer share and insider share carry overlapping information, so removing one group lets the others compensate.

### 5.7 Robustness (H4)
Decision rule: H4 is supported if the ordering prevalence < causal-reputation rule < reference learner holds for every label variant, and if the gap between the reference learner and the best rule keeps its sign.

The ordering holds for all six label variants (Table 5; results/table6_robustness.md), and the learner–rule gap stays positive, ranging from 0.052 (rug within 3,600 s) to 0.548 (DRAIN only). **H4 is supported.** In the robustness runs the rule is the causal-reputation rule; the other rules were not re-run per label. The gap is largest for DRAIN only (0.933 versus 0.385; lift 3.56) and for the alternative *collapse90* label (0.942 versus 0.590). Both labels are defined from the reserve path, so the reputation rule is a weak comparator for them. A one-feature rule on reserves at the snapshot reaches 0.907 for DRAIN and 0.919 for collapse90, and LightGBM beats it by only 0.026 [0.022, 0.030] and 0.023 [0.019, 0.026] (post hoc; Table 6). The ordering in H4 holds, but the large gaps in Table 5 measure how weak reputation is for reserve-defined labels, not how much the learner adds.

Other variations change little. Excluding launches with a capture silence in the first 3 s (61,574 test launches) or excluding the multi-hop seam window from training leaves PR-AUC at 0.953. Five seeds give 0.9528 ± 0.0001; the seed changes only LightGBM's row and column subsampling, since splits and folds are fixed. Training on the sampled legacy features (`harness_sample`; 346,189 launches, rug prevalence 0.800; results/E5_legacy_selection_bias.csv) and testing on the complete-decoder hold-out costs 0.008 PR-AUC (0.945) and triples ECE (0.019). The release contains no `tape_replay` basis, so the replayed-feature arm could not be run.

Two variations do matter. For **first-time creators** (12,823 hold-out launches, prevalence 0.588), the reputation rule is by construction no better than prevalence (0.588). LightGBM still reaches 0.868 (lift 1.48, ROC-AUC 0.861), against 0.962 for repeat creators. For the **regime shift**, a model trained on classic launches ranks mayhem launches poorly (ROC-AUC 0.735) and is badly calibrated on them (ECE 0.075). A model trained on mayhem launches does better (ROC-AUC 0.819, ECE 0.023). The classic-trained ROC-AUC has a creator-clustered interval of [0.683, 0.784]. Training on classic and mayhem launches together gives 0.831 [0.805, 0.851] and an ECE of 0.014, so the failure comes from training on one mode only. Mayhem PR-AUC stays high in both cases (0.947 and 0.965) only because prevalence is 0.872.

### 5.8 Do explanations survive the leakage controls?
The permutation-importance rankings under P1 and P4 (published reputation features) are similar but not identical: Kendall's τ = 0.74 over 13 features (results/E6_permutation_importance.csv, results/E6_summary.json, Figure 5). Under P1 the top features are the published rug share (0.040 PR-AUC drop), developer buy size (0.030) and the published launch count (0.019). Under P4, developer buy size moves to the top (0.047), followed by the rug share (0.044) and creator share at the snapshot (0.025). The mayhem flag loses all importance (0.006 to 0.000), and the missing-history indicator doubles (0.006 to 0.012). For unseen creators, then, the model relies more on what the creator does at launch and less on platform mode. Explanations computed under random splits would mis-rank these features for exactly the population a protective tool targets.

### 5.9 Lead time, non-trivial launches and operating points
Internal review raised three questions about how useful the scores are at decision time. We answered them after the main analysis with the pre-specified reference learner, seed and bootstrap (post hoc; code/s06_review_r6.py, results/R6_review_analyses.csv, results/table7_review_r6.md). Table 6 gives the ranking results and Table 7 the operating points.

**Lead time.** Many rugs follow the snapshot closely. Among the 42,393 hold-out rugs, the median time from snapshot to rug is 25.7 s; 27.3% occur within 10 s and 54.4% within 30 s. When rugs within 30 s or 60 s of the snapshot are dropped and all non-rugs kept, PR-AUC falls to 0.861 (prevalence 0.499) and 0.746 (prevalence 0.376), and ROC-AUC to 0.886 and 0.865. **Non-trivial launches.** On the 29,740 launches whose reserves peaked at 1 SOL or more, PR-AUC stays at 0.956 but ROC-AUC drops to 0.819, and to 0.780 at 5 SOL. Weighting each launch by its peak reserves, a rough measure of SOL at risk, gives a PR-AUC of 0.897 against a weighted prevalence of 0.770. Ranking is weaker among the launches that attracted real money.

**Operating points fixed on validation days.** The MCC in Table 4 uses a threshold set from the test scores. Setting it on the two validation days instead, with the model refitted without them, gives an MCC of 0.654 against 0.660. That threshold flags two thirds of the hold-out and 63.8% of its 483 graduated tokens, so it is not a usable blocking rule. A 1% budget set on the validation days flags 1.9% of test launches with a precision of 0.997 and no graduated token, but it catches only 2.7% of rugs. The low end of the score distribution is more useful. On the P4 hold-out, the 1% of launches with the lowest scores contain 0.3% rugs, against 4.5% for the insider rule and a prevalence of 58.8%. The PR-AUC of the non-rug class is 0.906, against 0.794 for the insider rule and 0.412 for prevalence. The model is better at marking a small set of launches as probably safe than at blocking most rugs.

## 6. Discussion
**What the protocol ladder measures.** The P1–P2 gap isolates creator identity, the P2–P3 gap isolates temporal drift, and P4 adds the restriction to new wallets. On this table, with causal features, the first two gaps are essentially zero (0.957, 0.958, 0.953), and almost the entire drop (to 0.923) comes from scoring unseen creators. The 26-day window shows no temporal decay either (Figure 4). The leakage that random splits introduce here is therefore not a dramatic artefact of memorised creator identity. It is a population effect (Section 5.2: inside the random split, launches by unseen creators score 0.898): the average launch in a random split comes from a serial creator with a long, informative history, whereas the launches a protective tool most needs to judge come from new wallets, which rug less often and are harder to rank. The gap is modest in absolute terms, but it is systematic: P1 lies above P4 in all five folds. We recommend that future pump.fun studies report P4 alongside any random-split result, together with the test prevalence, because PR-AUC is not comparable across populations with different base rates.

**Reputation is powerful and dangerous.** A creator's track record is a legitimate signal: serial ruggers exist, and wallets cost almost nothing. A track record is causal only if it is computed as of the launch. The embargoed sweep gives a cheap reference implementation. Our audit gives a reassuring answer for this release: the published rug share behaves like a causal quantity, agreeing with the causal reconstruction far more often than with the look-ahead one, and it carries no hidden performance advantage. The look-ahead variant gained a detectable but small 0.0008 PR-AUC, so on a 300-second label, outcomes that are not yet knowable at launch add little beyond a creator's resolved history. The result might differ for slower labels, or if look-ahead entered other fields. The count field could not be reproduced exactly, so users should treat `f10_rep_n` as defined by the provider rather than recompute-able.

**Rules before models.** If a one-feature rule came within the clustered interval of the reference learner, the operational recommendation would be the rule, which is transparent, cheap and harder to game silently (Rudin, 2019). That is not the case here. The best rule trails LightGBM by 0.054 PR-AUC overall and by 0.107 on unseen creators, and for first-time creators the reputation rule carries no information at all, while the learner still reaches 0.868. The choice among tree learners, by contrast, is immaterial (differences ≤ 0.002). The one-feature reputation rule remains a strong, interpretable baseline for repeat creators (0.932). At a 1% alert budget, the setting in which a wallet would actually block a purchase, it reaches a precision of 0.978 against 1.000 for the learner. Both figures use budgets set on test scores. With the budget fixed on validation days the learner's precision is 0.997, but it then flags only 2.7% of rugs (Section 5.9).

**Adversarial adaptation.** Every decision-time feature is under the creator's control to some degree, as Table 8 summarises: fresh wallets defeat reputation, and bundle splitting hides insider share. Section 5.7's results on first-time creators bound what remains when reputation is unavailable: about 0.87 PR-AUC at prevalence 0.59. Under P4, developer buy size and creator share become the most important features, and both can be shaped by an attacker at some cost. We saw no drift over 12 days, but the window is short. The poor transfer from classic to mayhem launches shows that a platform-level change in launch mechanics can break a detector abruptly. Detection should therefore be read as a moving target, and models should be re-validated after every platform change.

| Feature group | Who controls it | Evasion route | Cost to the attacker |
|---|---|---|---|

**Unseen wallets are not unseen entities.** P4 holds out creator *wallets*, not the people behind them. An operator who rotates wallets appears as many first-time creators. Some of their behavioural signature may still leak through flow and holder features, and bundle linkage of the kind released with MELT (Hu et al., 2026) would be needed to hold out entities. Szwajcok et al. (2026) document large-scale manipulation on pump.fun by strategic actors with paid third-party tools, and such actors can spread their launches over many wallets. P4 is therefore the strictest protocol available on this table, not a guarantee of entity-disjointness.

**Implications for wallets, launchpads and researchers.** Calibrated probabilities allow alert budgets instead of a single threshold. Since most rugs leave within half a minute of the snapshot, a wallet gains more from the low end of the score distribution, a short list of launches that are probably not rugs, than from blocking at the high end. A launchpad sees the same features earlier and can act on creators. Rate-limiting wallets whose resolved history is mostly rugs is the use for which the causal reputation is built. Studies should report the protocol, the label rule, the data revision, the lead-time distribution and thresholds fixed on validation data.

## 7. Limitations
- Labels are rule-based observations of price and holding paths, not adjudicated fraud. Some legitimate launches that collapse are labelled rugged, and some exits below the thresholds are not.
- The dataset is produced by a single automated third-party pipeline, and collector configuration alone can change which launches a study observes (Kamat, 2026b). We cannot independently re-derive it from raw chain data within this study, so errors in its decoder or capture would propagate. We mitigate this with seam, silence and sampling analyses but cannot rule it out.
- The study window is 26 days of complete-decoder data (53 days including the legacy subset). Longer-run drift, especially after platform rule changes, is untested.
- Decision-time features are limited to those published in the snapshot. Richer trace features such as those in MELT could change the comparison between learners and rules.
- The embargo E is a modelling choice. E2b varies it between 600 s and 86,400 s, and the learner is almost insensitive (0.954 to 0.952 PR-AUC) while the rule is not (0.909 to 0.871). Other definitions of when an outcome is knowable are possible.
- Rug labels and features were not re-derived from the chain. Our RPC cross-check covers creator identity, timing and token metadata for eight launches only.
- The legacy release contains only sampled (`harness_sample`) features, so the comparison between replayed and sampled features planned in Section 4.6 could not be made. The legacy training run tests transfer from sampled features only.
- Several analyses were added after the main run during internal review (Table 9). They use the pre-specified models, seed and bootstrap, but they were not in the original test families and are reported as post hoc.
- Creator wallets are not entities (Section 6), so P4 may still contain entity-level leakage.
- We evaluate ranking, calibration and alert precision, not avoided monetary loss. A loss-based evaluation would need trade-level entry and exit prices that the snapshot table does not provide.
- Hypotheses, decision rules, hyperparameters and protocols were fixed in code before the first full run. After that run, internal review found implementation errors that we corrected before the reported run (Table 9). The study was not registered with a third party.
- The snapshot moment is set by the data provider's capture rule, so its age at decision time varies across launches. Section 5.9 reports how performance depends on the time left before the rug.
- Creator histories start with the first launch in the release. A wallet that was active before then enters with a shorter history than it has.
- Detection results can be used by attackers to evade detectors. We release no per-wallet scores.

## 8. Conclusion
Using a public table of about 2.1 million pump.fun launches (924,742 after cleaning), we showed three things. First, random-mint splits overstate early rug-pull detection only modestly (PR-AUC 0.957 versus 0.923 on unseen creators). The gap comes from who is tested: inside the random split, launches by creators absent from training score 0.898, so new creators are harder under any protocol. Second, the published creator reputation shows no look-ahead: it matches a causal reconstruction better than a look-ahead one, also on the launches where the two differ, and gives no performance gain, although a true look-ahead reconstruction would add a small 0.0008 PR-AUC. Third, a gradient-boosting learner beats every one-feature rule, by 0.107 PR-AUC on unseen creators. These conclusions hold across six label definitions, capture filters, seeds and 12 days of frozen-model scoring. A model trained on classic launches does not carry over to mayhem-mode launches, although pooled training does. Half of the rugs come within 26 s of the snapshot, and for rugs at least a minute away PR-AUC falls to 0.746 at prevalence 0.376. Early rug-pull detection on launchpads should be evaluated time-forward on unseen creators, with causal track records, against simple rules, with thresholds fixed on validation data, and with prevalence, lead time and calibration reported.
